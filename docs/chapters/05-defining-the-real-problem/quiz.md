#### 1. According to Chapter 5, what is a problem definition?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The practice of stating, in specific and falsifiable terms, the difficulty a target customer experiences that the venture intends to address
2. A short slogan used to advertise the eventual solution to potential investors
3. Any complaint a founder overhears from a stranger, whether or not it relates to the target customer
4. The founder's best guess at what customers might want, updated only after the business launches
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A problem definition is the practice of stating, in specific and falsifiable terms, the difficulty your target customer experiences that your venture intends to address — specific enough that real evidence could confirm or contradict it. This is the second block on the canvas, and it only makes sense for a specific target customer, not people in general, which is why a vague observation like "people are busy" fails to qualify.

#### 2. How does the chapter define a pain point?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The general negative experience — frustration, cost, wasted time — that results from any unsolved problem
2. One specific, nameable instance of a customer's pain, concrete enough to describe in a single sentence
3. The finished sentence that will occupy the Problem block of the canvas
4. A requirement that no existing option, including doing nothing, adequately satisfies
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A pain point is one specific, nameable instance of a customer's pain, concrete enough to describe in a single sentence — for example, Jordan's customer losing an unpaid afternoon of work to drive forty minutes each way for grooming drop-off. It is narrower than customer pain, which is the general negative experience behind it, and different from a problem statement or an unmet need.

#### 3. Which statement best distinguishes customer pain from a pain point, as the chapter uses the terms?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Customer pain is always financial, while a pain point can be any type of frustration
2. A pain point is a broad category, while customer pain is one specific instance inside it
3. Customer pain is the negative experience a customer feels overall, while a pain point is one specific, single-sentence instance of that experience

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Customer pain is the negative experience — frustration, cost, wasted time, embarrassment, or risk — a customer feels as a direct result of an unsolved problem. A pain point narrows that broader experience down to one specific, nameable instance concrete enough to state in a single sentence. The two terms describe different levels of specificity, not interchangeable synonyms.

#### 4. Priya's interviewees kept saying "I don't have time to cook." Why does the chapter treat this as something other than the root cause?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

A solution built for the symptom alone would have missed what was actually producing the complaint.

#### 6. What does problem severity measure?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

3. How soon the customer wants the problem solved once it is confirmed real

</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Problem severity measures how painful or costly the problem is on any single occurrence — independent of how often it happens or how soon the customer wants it fixed. A flooded basement is an example of high severity paired with low frequency, showing severity is its own distinct dimension from problem frequency and problem urgency.

#### 8. A founder finishes a round of interviews with three candidate problems in view. What does the chapter say problem prioritization requires the founder to do?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Pursue whichever problem was mentioned first in the very first interview
2. Rank the candidate problems using severity, frequency, and urgency evidence, then choose which to pursue first
3. Build a small solution for all three problems simultaneously to see which one sells

</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Problem prioritization is the deliberate act of ranking candidate problems and choosing which one to pursue first, using severity, frequency, and urgency evidence rather than personal preference or the order problems happened to surface. Founders commonly surface more than one candidate problem after a full round of interviews, making this ranking step necessary.

#### 9. What makes a problem a "real problem" rather than merely a perceived one, according to the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It has been described in a formal problem statement using the canvas's exact wording
2. It affects a large enough number of people to matter to investors
3. A founder is personally confident, based on outside research, that it exists
4. Customers actually experience it and can describe it from their own lived frustration when asked directly, confirmed through evidence
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. A real problem is one that customers actually experience and can describe from their own lived frustration when asked directly, confirmed through evidence rather than founder intuition. This distinguishes it from a perceived problem, which a founder believes exists based on logic or outside research but has not yet confirmed with the actual target customer.

#### 10. How does the chapter define a perceived problem?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A problem that has already failed a round of problem validation and should be discarded
2. A problem invented purely for the purpose of a pitch competition
3. A problem a founder believes customers have, based on logic, outside research, or personal experience, that hasn't yet been confirmed by talking to the target customer
4. A problem that only exists in a market survey and has no basis in individual experience
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A perceived problem is one a founder believes customers have, based on logic, outside research, or personal experience, that has not yet been confirmed by talking to the actual target customer. The chapter stresses that every real problem starts out perceived — the mistake is building a solution around it before validation, not holding the perception itself.

Problem validation is the process of testing a perceived problem against real customer conversations and behavior to confirm whether it is genuinely felt, exactly as urgently as assumed. Jordan's untested assumption about drop-off frustration remains perceived until real customers confirm it directly — logic and outside research alone cannot make a perceived problem real.

#### 19. A customer agrees a problem is real and even mildly bothersome, but says they've never paid for anything to address it because "rent and groceries come first." Which concept best explains this pattern?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Problem urgency, because the customer does not want the problem solved quickly
2. Essential bills priority — the tendency to direct limited money toward non-negotiable obligations before any discretionary spending
3. Problem-solution fit, because no available solution matches the customer's root cause
4. Perceived problem, because the customer has not actually experienced the difficulty firsthand
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Essential bills priority is the well-documented tendency for customers to direct limited money first toward non-negotiable obligations — rent, groceries, utilities, debt — before any discretionary spending on a problem that, however real, doesn't threaten their basic stability. It is one specific, common reason a confirmed real problem still fails to produce willingness to solve.
