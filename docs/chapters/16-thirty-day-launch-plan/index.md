## The 30-Day Launch Plan

A **thirty-day launch plan** is a concrete, week-by-week roadmap covering the 30 days immediately following the course, translating a completed canvas into specific, scheduled actions rather than leaving validated ideas sitting on a page. That plan takes the shape of a **launch roadmap**: the overall structure organizing 30 days of action into a sequence, replacing the multi-year theoretical business plan this course deliberately avoids, from Chapter 1, with something short enough to actually follow and revise.

## Three Questions Every Plan Answers

At its core, the thirty-day launch plan answers three questions this course's own learning outcomes name directly. **What to sell** restates the minimum testable iteration from Chapter 10 as a concrete, sellable offer for the next 30 days — not the eventual full vision, but exactly what's being sold starting Monday. **Who to sell to** restates the target customer from Chapter 4 as a specific, findable group for these 30 days. **How to reach them** restates the distribution channels from Chapter 13 as the actual, chosen methods that will be used, not every method that was considered.

Answering these three questions is mostly assembly, not new work — the thirty-day launch plan's job is to pull the sharpest, most validated answer to each one from the chapters already completed, rather than inventing anything new.

## Weekly Milestones and Targets

A 30-day plan is too long to hold in view all at once, so it gets broken into a **weekly milestone**: a specific, meaningful checkpoint the plan aims to reach by the end of a given week — not a vague hope, but a concrete marker like "10 households contacted" or "first paid booking confirmed." Each milestone is measured against a **weekly target**: the specific number attached to that milestone, set in advance so progress can be checked objectively rather than by feeling.

## Launch Readiness

By the end of the 30 days, a founder checks for **launch readiness**: a specific, evaluated state confirming the venture has enough validated demand, a workable offer, and a functioning way to reach customers to continue operating beyond the plan's initial 30 days — not perfection, just enough real evidence to keep going with confidence.

## Momentum, Setbacks, and Reviewing Wins and Lessons

Thirty days rarely goes exactly as planned, and this course builds in room for that directly. **Momentum building** is the deliberate practice of stacking small wins visibly across the 30 days — a first booking, a first referral — so progress stays motivating even before the venture is fully proven. **Setback normalization** is treating a missed weekly target or a failed test as an expected, normal part of the plan rather than a sign something has gone wrong — directly extending the founder resilience and growth mindset built back in Chapter 2. A **wins and lessons review** is a structured weekly reflection, ideally shared with cohort peers, that names what worked, what didn't, and what changes for next week — turning both wins and setbacks into forward motion rather than letting either one pass unexamined.

## When the Plan Needs to Bend

Sometimes a weekly milestone gets missed for a specific, fixable reason, and the plan needs a small deliberate adjustment rather than a full pivot back in Chapter 11's sense. A **time-saving pivot** is a small adjustment to the plan that reduces how much founder time a step requires — simplifying a booking process, batching driveway sessions by neighborhood to cut driving time. A **money-saving pivot** is a small adjustment that reduces what a step costs — delaying a planned purchase, switching to a cheaper supplier for one ongoing cost.

    Here's a shortcut for choosing between the two: if you're missing targets because there aren't enough hours in the week, look for a time-saving pivot. If you're missing them because spending is outpacing the startup budget from Chapter 15, look for a money-saving pivot. Naming which resource is actually scarce points straight at the fix.
