## The CO.STARTERS Canvas: One Page, Eleven Questions

The **CO.STARTERS Canvas** is a single-page business-design tool made up of 11 linked sections — Customer, Problem, Solution, Alternatives, Benefit, Advantage, Message, Distribution, Revenue, Startup Needs, and Costs — each holding the answer to one specific, plain-language question about a venture. Unlike a multi-page business plan, the entire canvas fits on one sheet, which is a deliberate design choice: a founder should be able to see their whole business logic at a glance, and a peer should be able to review it in under two minutes during a cohort session.

The canvas is the tool this entire course is organized around. Every Field Discovery Window from Chapter 2 exists to gather evidence for a specific block. Every live session updates one or more blocks based on what that evidence showed. By Session 4, described in Chapter 17, a completed canvas becomes the backbone of your 2-minute pitch. Nothing in the remaining 14 chapters happens outside the structure this one page provides.

Two properties of the canvas matter before you look at your first block. First, it is a **living document** — every block you fill in Session 1 is expected to change as real-world evidence arrives, not a final answer to defend. Second, the blocks are **connected**, not independent: a weak answer in the Problem block cascades into a weak answer in Solution, which cascades into a weak Message. Reading the canvas as 11 separate homework questions misses the point; reading it as one interlocking argument is the whole point.

## How This Canvas Compares to the Business Model Canvas

If you've encountered startup tools before, you may recognize the shape of this page. The **Business Model Canvas** is a widely used strategic-planning tool, developed for established and scaling companies, that maps nine building blocks — including key partners, key activities, and cost structure — describing how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value.

The CO.STARTERS Canvas is a deliberate simplification of that idea for a specific, narrower moment: the early-stage venture defined in Chapter 1, where there is no operating business yet to map — only an unproven idea to test. Where the Business Model Canvas assumes a business model roughly exists and asks you to describe it, the CO.STARTERS Canvas assumes almost nothing exists yet and asks you to define it, block by block, from a blank page. Two blocks make this difference obvious: Advantage (why you, specifically, are positioned to solve this) and Startup Needs (what you need to acquire once before you can even begin) have no direct equivalent on the Business Model Canvas, because they only matter to a founder who hasn't started yet.

## Canvas Blocks and the Canvas Sprint

Each of the 11 sections on the page is called a **canvas block**: a single labeled section of the CO.STARTERS Canvas that holds a short, specific answer to one guiding question, deliberately scoped to fit in the space of a large index card rather than a full paragraph. The size constraint is intentional — a block that needs three paragraphs to answer usually means the founder hasn't yet reached a clear enough answer, not that the tool is too small.

Blocks get filled during a **canvas sprint**: a timed, focused group exercise — typically 5 to 10 minutes per block — in which cohort participants draft an answer individually, then briefly compare it with a peer before moving to the next block. The time limit is a feature, not a shortcut: a strict clock forces a founder to write their honest first-pass answer instead of endlessly polishing one block while the rest of the canvas sits empty, which is exactly the idea-trap behavior Chapter 1 warned against.

## Walking the Eleven Blocks

With the full page in view, here is what each block asks — in the order they appear on the canvas, which is also roughly the order the rest of this book addresses them.

The first two blocks establish who you're serving and why it matters to them. The **Customer Block** holds a specific, falsifiable description of who the venture serves — not "everyone with a dog," but a defined group like Jordan's "busy dog owners within 5 miles." Chapter 4 is devoted entirely to filling this block well. The **Problem Block** holds the specific pain that customer group feels, stated from their point of view rather than the founder's — Chapter 5 covers this in depth, including how to tell a real problem from one a founder only assumes exists.

The next four blocks turn that problem into a market position. The **Solution Block** holds what the venture will actually offer to address the problem — covered fully in Chapter 6, including how to keep a first solution deliberately small. The **Alternatives Block** holds how the customer solves this problem today without you, including doing nothing at all — the subject of Chapter 7. The **Benefit Block** holds why the customer wants your solution specifically, in terms of their own outcome rather than your product's features — and the **Advantage Block** holds why you, this particular founder, are positioned to deliver it better than anyone else could. Chapter 8 covers both blocks together, since a strong value proposition needs both halves.

The following three blocks turn that market position into something sellable. The **Message Block** holds the one-line way you'll describe the offer to a stranger — covered in Chapter 12. The **Distribution Block** holds how customers will actually discover and reach the offer — covered in Chapter 13. The **Revenue Block** holds how the venture will charge for what it delivers — covered in Chapter 14.

The final two blocks ground the whole page in real numbers. The **Startup Needs Block** holds the one-time resources required before the venture can begin at all — equipment, a license, a first batch of supplies — covered in Chapter 15 alongside its neighbor, the **Costs Block**, which holds the ongoing expenses the venture will carry once it's running, such as materials, gas, or subscriptions.

## Canvas Synthesis: From Eleven Answers to One Story

Filling in 11 blocks correctly is necessary but not sufficient. **Canvas synthesis** is the practice of reading all 11 completed blocks together as a single, coherent argument for why the venture should exist — checking that each block's answer actually supports its neighbors rather than contradicting them.

A canvas can have 11 individually reasonable-sounding answers that still don't add up. If the Customer block names "busy working parents" but the Distribution block says "flyers posted at the senior center," the canvas doesn't hang together — the message will never reach the customer it claims to be for. Synthesis is the step where a founder, usually with a peer's help during a cohort session, reads straight across the page looking for exactly this kind of mismatch. It happens naturally throughout the course, but it becomes explicit and deliberate in Session 4, when the full canvas has to support a single 2-minute pitch, covered in Chapter 17.

!!! mascot-warning "Don't Treat Blocks as 11 Separate Homework Questions"
    ![Scout warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A common trap here is answering each block in isolation — writing a technically correct Revenue block that has nothing to do with the Customer block right next to it. The fix: after every sprint, read your canvas left to right, out loud, as one sentence. If it doesn't flow as a single argument, a block needs revising, not just filling in.

## Key Takeaways

Before Chapter 4 puts the first block under a microscope, hold onto the shape of the whole page:

- The **CO.STARTERS Canvas** is one page, 11 blocks, purpose-built for an unproven early-stage venture — distinct from the **Business Model Canvas** it's descended from, which assumes an operating business already exists.
- A **canvas block** is a short, specific answer sized to fit an index card; a **canvas sprint** is the timed exercise that forces a first honest draft instead of endless polishing.
- The 11 blocks — **Customer, Problem, Solution, Alternatives, Benefit, Advantage, Message, Distribution, Revenue, Startup Needs,** and **Costs** — move in order from who you serve, to what you offer, to how it reaches and sustains itself.
- **Canvas synthesis** is reading all 11 answers together as one argument, not 11 separate homework questions — a discipline this course returns to at every session.
