# References: Interpreting Validation Evidence

1. [Lean startup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_startup) - Wikipedia - Covers the build-measure-learn loop and the formal distinction between a pivot (a structured course correction) and continuing on the current path, the central decision this chapter teaches how to make.

2. [Confirmation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias) - Wikipedia - Explains the tendency to search for and interpret evidence in ways that confirm existing beliefs. Directly relevant to this chapter's core warning against reading a test result the way you hoped it would turn out.

3. [Falsifiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsifiability) - Wikipedia - Covers Karl Popper's principle that a claim is only scientifically meaningful if evidence could prove it wrong. Clarifies why this chapter insists a test hypothesis must be a specific, falsifiable prediction.

4. The Lean Startup (2011) - Eric Ries - Crown Business - Ries originated the build-measure-learn loop and formalized the pivot-or-persevere decision meeting as a recurring checkpoint, which is the direct source of this chapter's persevere, pivot, and kill framework.

5. The Mom Test (2013) - Rob Fitzpatrick - Robfitz Ltd - Fitzpatrick is credited with the technique of distinguishing what people actually do from the polite, non-committal things they say, clarifying why this chapter treats a customer payment signal as categorically stronger evidence than a compliment.

6. [Confirmation Bias in UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-bias-ux/) - Nielsen Norman Group - Explains how researchers unintentionally favor data that supports their existing assumptions, and how to reduce that bias, reinforcing the chapter's discipline of reading evidence honestly.

7. [Do Pivots Matter?](https://steveblank.com/2014/01/14/whats-a-pivot/) - Steve Blank - Argues that a genuine pivot means changing a specific business model component, not just the product, echoing this chapter's definition of a pivot as changing one significant element while keeping what evidence has supported.

8. [The Discipline of Business Experimentation](https://hbr.org/2014/12/the-discipline-of-business-experimentation) - Harvard Business Review - Shows how companies misread test results as statistical noise rather than real signal, illustrating why this chapter separates a raw test result from its interpretation.

9. [Don't Build When You Build-Measure-Learn](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/dont-build-when-you-build-measure-learn) - Strategyzer - Explains how to achieve maximum learning for minimum effort using low-cost MVP formats, directly supporting this chapter's concepts of the smallest testable version and low-capital testing.

10. [Act Like a Startup](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/act-like-a-startup/) - MIT Sloan Management Review - Describes how startups learn as quickly and inexpensively as possible and embrace pivots rather than doubling down on a failing plan, reinforcing rapid experimentation and low-capital testing.
