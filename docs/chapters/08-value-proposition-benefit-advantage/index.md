## Two Questions, One Pitch

Chapter 7 mapped the gap your offer fills. This chapter turns that gap into an actual pitch, built from two canvas blocks that only make sense together: Benefit and Advantage. A **value proposition** is the combined, specific answer to two separate questions — why the customer wants this solution, and why this founder is the right one to deliver it — stated together as a single, coherent claim. Most weak pitches answer only one of those two questions. This chapter makes sure you can answer both.

    This chapter splits neatly into two halves: what the customer gets, and why you specifically are the one who can deliver it. Most pitches lean hard on one half and forget the other — by the end, you'll have both. Let's find out for real!

## Benefit: Why the Customer Wants It

The Benefit block answers **why customer wants it**: the specific positive outcome your target customer experiences by choosing your solution, expressed in terms of their life rather than your product's features — echoing the feature-vs-benefit distinction from Chapter 6, now built out fully. The umbrella term for this is **customer benefit**: the value a customer receives from a solution, independent of how that value gets delivered.

Not every benefit lands the same way. A **functional benefit** is a practical, measurable outcome — time saved, money saved, a task completed. An **emotional benefit** is a feeling the customer gains — relief, confidence, pride, reduced guilt. Most real offers deliver both at once: Jordan's driveway grooming saves a functional 40 minutes of driving, and delivers the emotional benefit of not feeling like a neglectful pet owner. Among any list of benefits, the **core benefit** is the single one that matters most to the target customer — the one that, if removed, would make the customer stop caring about the rest. **Benefit articulation** is the discipline of stating that core benefit plainly, in the customer's own words, rather than in internal company language.

## Advantage: Why You're the One to Deliver It

The second half of the value proposition answers a different question entirely: **why you solve the problem** — not why the solution is good, but why *this specific founder* is positioned to deliver it well. **Founder advantage** is the combination of experience, relationships, skills, timing, or circumstance that makes a particular founder unusually well suited to solve a particular problem for a particular customer.

Three ingredients typically build that advantage. **Domain expertise** is direct knowledge or experience in the specific problem area, gained through work, study, or lived experience. **Founder credibility** is the evidence a founder can point to — a track record, a relevant background, or direct proof like Jordan's three successful driveway sessions — that makes their claim of advantage believable rather than self-declared. Together, these help answer whether a founder is the **best person to solve it**: whether, among everyone who could plausibly attempt this venture, this founder has a genuinely stronger claim than an average competitor would. **Advantage articulation** is stating that claim specifically — not "I care a lot about dogs," but "I've spent three years as a veterinary tech and already have the trust of a dozen local dog owners."

!!! mascot-thinking "A Great Benefit With No Advantage Is Just a Good Idea"
    ![Scout thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Notice something important here: a wonderful customer benefit, on its own, isn't a reason to bet on *this* founder — good ideas are cheap and anyone could copy one. Advantage is what makes an idea specifically yours to execute. Without it, you've described an opportunity for whoever gets there first, not necessarily for you.

## How Strong Is Your Advantage?

Not every founder advantage is equally durable. A **competitive advantage** is any factor that makes your offer harder for a rival to match — it could be as ordinary as having gotten there first. An **unfair advantage** is a stronger, harder-to-replicate version: something a competitor genuinely cannot easily copy, no matter how much they want to — an existing relationship, a proprietary process, or hard-won credibility built over years. **Advantage sustainability** asks how long that edge will actually hold once competitors notice and try to respond.

| Advantage Type | Question It Answers | How Easily Copied |
|-----------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Competitive Advantage | What edge do I currently have? | Sometimes easily copied |
| Unfair Advantage | What edge can't a rival easily copy? | Hard to copy |
| Advantage Sustainability | How long will this edge last? | Depends on the type above |

!!! mascot-tip "Look for What Took You Years, Not Weeks"
    ![Scout giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Here's a shortcut for finding your unfair advantage: list everything that took you years to build — relationships, reputation, a specific skill, direct experience with the exact problem. A competitor with a few weeks and some capital can copy almost anything else on your list, but not that.

## Writing the Value Proposition

With both halves in hand, a founder writes a **value statement**: the single, concise sentence combining benefit and advantage into one claim, typically following a pattern like "[Target customer] gets [core benefit] because [founder advantage], unlike [main alternative]." Jordan's value statement: "Busy dog owners get grooming without losing an afternoon, from someone they already trust in the neighborhood — unlike the booked-solid groomer across town."

A strong value statement earns three more properties. **Unique value** is whatever part of that combined claim no mapped alternative from Chapter 7 can currently offer. A **unique selling point** is the single most compelling piece of that unique value, chosen specifically for use in marketing messages — covered fully in Chapter 12. **Value clarity** means the statement is understandable on a single read, with no jargon a target customer wouldn't use themselves. **Value differentiation** is how clearly the statement sets your offer apart from every alternative mapped in Chapter 7 — a value proposition that could describe any competitor equally well has no real differentiation at all.

!!! mascot-warning "Don't Settle for 'High Quality, Great Service'"
    ![Scout warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A common trap here is writing a value statement so generic it could belong to any business in any industry — "high quality, great service, affordable prices." The fix: if a direct competitor could say the exact same sentence about their own business, it isn't a value proposition yet. Go back to your specific benefit and specific advantage and use their actual words.

## Refining Your Value Proposition

A first value statement is rarely the final one. **Value proposition refinement** is the ongoing process of tightening the value statement as new field evidence arrives — sharper language once you hear how customers actually describe the benefit themselves, or a stronger advantage claim once new credibility is earned. Like the solution iteration from Chapter 6, this isn't a one-time writing exercise; it's a statement that keeps getting truer as evidence accumulates.

## Key Takeaways

Before Chapter 9 tests these claims directly against real customers, hold onto both halves of the pitch:

- A **value proposition** combines **why customer wants it** — a **customer benefit**, whether **functional**, **emotional**, or the single **core benefit** — with **why you solve the problem**.
- **Founder advantage** rests on **domain expertise** and **founder credibility**, answering whether you're the **best person to solve it**, made concrete through **advantage articulation**.
- A **competitive advantage** becomes a truly **unfair advantage** when it has real **advantage sustainability** — hard for a rival to copy, not just currently absent.
- The finished **value statement** should show genuine **unique value**, a clear **unique selling point**, strong **value clarity**, and real **value differentiation** — refined continuously through **value proposition refinement**.
