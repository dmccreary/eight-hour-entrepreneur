#### 1. How does the chapter define an alternative solution?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Anything a target customer currently uses, does, or chooses instead of your proposed offer to address their problem
2. Any business that has raised more funding than your own venture
3. A solution a founder plans to build after the current offer succeeds
4. The single most expensive option available to a target customer
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. An alternative solution is anything a target customer currently uses, does, or chooses instead of your proposed offer to address their problem — a category far broader than "other companies selling something similar." This broader definition is why the chapter warns founders against filling in the Alternatives block too narrowly, since the word "competitor" tempts people to think only of rival businesses.

#### 2. What counts as a competitor, according to the chapter's definition?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Only another registered business selling a product similar to your own
2. Any government regulation that makes a venture harder to launch
3. Any existing solution — business, product, or workaround — that a target customer might choose over your offer

</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A competitor is any existing solution — business, product, or workaround — that a target customer might choose over your offer. This definition deliberately extends beyond formal rival businesses to include informal workarounds and even the option of doing nothing, all of which a target customer might pick instead of the founder's offer.

#### 3. How does the competitive landscape differ from a single competitor, as the chapter uses the terms?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The competitive landscape refers only to competitors located within the founder's own geographic area
2. A competitor is one alternative a customer might choose; the competitive landscape is the full, organized picture of every alternative available to the target customer
3. The two terms are synonyms and can be used interchangeably throughout the chapter
4. The competitive landscape only includes direct competitors, while the term "competitor" includes all four categories
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A competitor is one specific alternative a customer might choose; the competitive landscape is the resulting full picture — every alternative a target customer has available, organized and understood together. Mapping individual competitors is a step toward building the broader competitive landscape, not the same thing as it.

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A substitute solution doesn't solve the exact same problem at all, but satisfies enough of the same underlying need that a customer might choose it anyway. A self-service dog wash doesn't groom the dog, but it addresses the "my dog needs attention" feeling behind the request, which is what makes it a substitute rather than a direct competitor.

#### 8. Why does the chapter call the do-nothing alternative "the actual default competitor a venture has to beat"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because it is always the cheapest paid option available to the customer
2. Because it costs the customer nothing to keep choosing, and customers simply tolerating the problem, unsolved, is a real and common option
3. Because it is the option every direct competitor eventually adopts once they run out of customers
4. Because regulators require every venture to compare itself against doing nothing before launching
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. #### 10. A founder maps every alternative available to their target customer and finds that none of them — not even the do-nothing alternative — actually delivers something customers consistently say they want. What has the founder identified?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A direct competitor, because customers are already aware of every option
2. Competitive intelligence, because the founder has been updating a ledger of information
3. A market gap — a genuine, customer-confirmed shortfall shared by every current alternative
4. Competitive positioning, because the founder has decided where the offer sits in customers' minds
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A market gap is a genuine, customer-confirmed shortfall shared by every current alternative — something the status quo consistently fails to deliver that a target customer actually wants. Finding that no mapped alternative, including doing nothing, delivers what customers say they want is exactly how a real market gap gets confirmed.

    **Concept Tested:** Market Gap

#### 11. How does competitive positioning differ from differentiation, as the chapter distinguishes the two?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Differentiation is decided by competitors, while competitive positioning is decided entirely by customers
2. The two terms describe the same activity and are used interchangeably in the chapter
3. Competitive positioning applies only to direct competitors, while differentiation applies to every category of alternative
4. Differentiation is what makes an offer meaningfully different from mapped alternatives, while competitive positioning is the deliberate choice of where that offer sits in a customer's mind relative to the full landscape
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Differentiation is what makes an offer meaningfully different from every mapped alternative, grounded in a specific gap; competitive positioning is the deliberate choice of where that offer sits relative to the full competitive landscape in a customer's mind — faster, cheaper, more personal, more convenient. Positioning builds on differentiation rather than replacing it.
