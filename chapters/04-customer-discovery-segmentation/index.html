    The First Block, in Detail¶

    Chapter 3 showed you the whole canvas at once. This chapter puts the first block — Customer — under a microscope, because every other block on the page inherits whatever precision or vagueness this one starts with. A fuzzy customer definition produces a fuzzy problem statement, a fuzzy solution, and eventually a marketing message that reaches no one in particular. Everything in this chapter exists to answer one question with real precision: who, exactly, are you building this for?

    Naming Your Customer

    By the end of this chapter you'll be able to describe your customer specifically enough that a stranger could picture them — not "everyone who might like this," but one real, findable kind of person. That specificity is the single highest-leverage move in this entire course. Let's find out for real!

    From Broad Market to Target Customer¶

    Every venture starts with some notion of a broad market: the entire population of people who could conceivably ever use a solution like yours, described in the widest possible terms — "dog owners," "working parents," "small business owners." A broad market is a legitimate starting point, but it is never a stopping point, because "dog owners" spans a millionaire with a show poodle and a college student with a rescue mutt, and no single message, price, or offer can serve both well.

    The next step down is a customer segment: a subgroup of a venture's actual prospective customers who share meaningful characteristics — age, income, location, behavior, or need — that make them likely to respond to a similar offer in a similar way. The general marketing-theory term for this same kind of grouping, applied to an entire market rather than to one venture's specific prospects, is a market segment — the two describe the same underlying idea, just at different scales. Narrowing further still produces a niche market: a segment small and specific enough that a single small venture could realistically become the obvious choice for everyone in it, rather than one option among hundreds. Jordan's broad market is "dog owners"; the customer segment is "dog owners without easy access to a groomer"; the niche is "dog owners within 5 miles who've expressed frustration with driving to get their dog groomed."

    Narrowing has a practical ceiling, defined by the serviceable market: the portion of a niche or segment that a specific venture, with its specific resources and location, could realistically reach and serve — as opposed to the theoretical total if resources were unlimited. Jordan's serviceable market isn't every frustrated dog owner in the country; it's the ones within driving distance of a single mobile groomer working alone.

    All of this narrowing exists to reach a target customer: the specific, well-defined group of people a venture has deliberately chosen to serve first, described precisely enough to guide every other decision on the canvas — who to interview, what to build, how to price it, and where to advertise. "Busy dog owners within 5 miles who don't have easy groomer access" is a target customer. "Dog owners" is not — it's still a broad market wearing a target customer's clothing.

    Priya went through the same narrowing with her meal-prep idea. Her broad market was "families who eat dinner." Segmenting by income, household structure, and daily schedule got her to "dual-income households with children under 10." Niching further, guided by what her own sister actually experienced, got her to a specific target customer: "dual-income parents of children under 10, working rotating or unpredictable shift schedules, within her metro delivery area."

    Narrowing Doesn't Shrink Your Business — It Sharpens It

    Notice the instinct to resist here: narrowing your customer feels like turning away paying business. It's the opposite. A message built for "dual-income parents on rotating shifts" will outperform a message built for "families" with every single family it reaches — including families outside the narrow definition who see themselves in the specific description anyway.

    Naming Who You Serve¶

    The course uses several closely related terms for the act of putting your target customer into words, and it's worth being precise about how they differ. Customer definition is the general practice of describing who a venture serves in clear, specific terms — the umbrella activity this whole chapter is teaching. Who you serve is simply the plain-language phrase the CO.STARTERS Canvas's Customer block itself asks you to answer. Customer identification is the research process of figuring out, through evidence rather than guesswork, which real people actually match your intended definition. And a target market definition is the written, canvas-ready output of that process — the finished sentence that goes in the Customer block.

    Read together, the four terms describe one continuous activity: you do customer identification (research) to reach a customer definition (clarity) about who you serve (the question), which you write down as a target market definition (the answer).

    From Segment to One Person: ICP, Persona, and Avatar¶

    A target customer describes a group. Three more tools exist to sharpen that group down to something a founder can picture and design for directly.

    An ideal customer profile describes the specific combination of firmographic or demographic traits — company size, industry, income, location, household type — that make someone the best possible fit for a venture, most often used for describing an organization or household type rather than a single individual. A customer persona is a semi-fictional but research-grounded composite character representing a target customer segment, given a name, a short backstory, and specific goals and frustrations, so a founder can reason about decisions ("would Dana want this?") instead of reasoning about an abstract segment. A customer avatar is the most visual and specific version of this idea: often a single illustrated or described individual standing in for the entire target customer group, used primarily in marketing and messaging work to keep language grounded in one real-feeling person rather than a statistic.

    Tool Best for Priya's Example
    Ideal Customer Profile Defining the traits that make someone a strong fit Dual-income household, children under 10, rotating shift work, within delivery zone
    Customer Persona Making decisions grounded in a realistic person "Dana," 34, ER nurse, two kids, values time over variety
    Customer Avatar Writing marketing copy that feels personal A single illustrated portrait of "Dana" used across ads and the website

    Early Adopters vs the Mainstream¶

    Not every target customer is equally reachable on day one. An early adopter is a customer who actively seeks out new solutions to a problem they already recognize they have, is willing to tolerate an imperfect first version, and often becomes a vocal advocate if it works. A mainstream customer, by contrast, waits for a solution to be proven, polished, and recommended by others before trying it themselves — they want evidence the early adopters already gathered.

    Sell to the Early Adopter First, on Purpose

    Here's a shortcut for an early-stage venture: don't design your first offer for the mainstream customer at all. Design it for the early adopter who already feels the pain acutely enough to try an unpolished first version — Jordan's first three driveway customers were early adopters, not a representative sample of "all dog owners."

    B2B or B2C? Two Different Games¶

    One more distinction shapes almost everything downstream: who actually makes the purchase decision. A B2B customer (business-to-business) is an organization — represented by one or more individual decision-makers — buying a solution to use within its own operations, typically involving longer sales cycles and multiple stakeholders. A B2C customer (business-to-consumer) is an individual person buying a solution for their own personal use, typically involving a single decision-maker and a much faster purchase decision. Both Jordan's and Priya's ventures are B2C — a single parent or pet owner decides and pays. A founder offering the same dog-grooming service to a chain of pet boutiques instead would be operating in a B2B context, with a different buyer, a different sales process, and a different canvas entirely.

    Understanding Your Customer¶

    Once a target customer is named, four more concepts describe what you still need to learn about them. Customer needs are the specific outcomes or resources a customer requires to solve their problem, whether or not they can articulate those needs clearly themselves. Customer behavior is what that customer actually does — the observable actions, habits, and choices they make today, which is frequently a more reliable signal than what they say. Customer motivation is the underlying reason driving that behavior — the emotional or practical "why" beneath an observable action. Customer journey is the sequence of steps a customer moves through from first becoming aware of a problem to eventually deciding whether to buy a solution to it.

    These four ideas connect in a specific, useful way:

    • A customer's stated need ("I want healthier dinners") is not always their real motivation ("I feel guilty when I can't cook").
    • Behavior is the tie-breaker: what someone actually orders, clicks, or pays for reveals more than what they say they want.
    • Mapping the customer journey — problem noticed, solution searched for, options compared, decision made — shows a founder exactly where in that sequence their message needs to land.

    Don't Trust Stated Needs Over Observed Behavior

    A common trap here is designing entirely around what customers say they need in an interview, while ignoring what they actually do. The fix: whenever the two conflict, weight behavior more heavily — six of Priya's interviewees said they "wanted variety," but every one of them had reordered the same three meals for a month.

    Choosing, Prioritizing, and Testing Your Customer Definition¶

    A founder rarely has just one plausible target customer in mind at the start. Customer prioritization is the deliberate act of ranking multiple candidate customer definitions and choosing which one to pursue first, based on how urgently they feel the problem, how easily they can be reached, and how many of them exist. Customer fit describes how well a specific customer definition actually matches what the venture can realistically deliver — a customer who needs same-day delivery is a poor fit for a founder who can only deliver on weekends, regardless of how much that customer wants the product.

    Every customer definition, no matter how carefully reasoned, starts life as a customer assumption: an unverified belief about who your customer is and what they want, following the exact same definition Chapter 1 gave for any assumption. "Busy dog owners will pay $40 for driveway grooming" was an assumption before Jordan tested it. Writing a sharp customer definition is not the finish line — it's the specific, testable claim the next Field Discovery Window from Chapter 2 exists to check.

    Key Takeaways¶

    Before Chapter 5 moves to the Problem block, hold onto how a customer gets defined:

    • A target customer emerges by narrowing a broad market through a market segment and niche market, bounded by what's realistically a serviceable market.
    • Customer definition, who you serve, customer identification, and target market definition describe one continuous process: research to clarity to a written answer.
    • An ideal customer profile, customer persona, and customer avatar sharpen a group into a specific, decision-ready person.
    • Early adopters are the right first customer for an early-stage venture — not the cautious mainstream customer, and not without knowing whether you're selling B2B or B2C.
    • Customer needs, behavior, motivation, and journey describe what to learn next; customer prioritization and customer fit decide who to pursue; and every definition remains a customer assumption until the field proves it.

    You Just Named Your Customer

    You now have the tools to describe your customer specifically enough that the rest of the canvas can actually point at someone real. That precision is what makes every later block sharper. Let's find out for real, starting with their problem in Chapter 5.
